Article 324, Form 6 and the 2026 Special Intensive Revision proceedings

Executive Summary

The Supreme Court of India has taken up a constitutional and administrative dispute over the framework of the electoral rolls. The INDIA bloc petition challenges the nationwide SIR and seeks a retired-Supreme-Court-judge-led audit committee, production of records, and interim suspension of the exercise. Related Form 6 and Delhi matters raise narrower but overlapping questions. The case sets two constitutional positions against each other: the opposition’s claim of administrative overreach causing systemic voter exclusion, and the ECI’s mandate under Article 324 to manage, clean and de-duplicate the electorate. Four live issues run through the case: a digital ancestral declaration attached to Form 6, alleged unilateral decision-making inside the three-member Commission, the mechanics of algorithmic deletion and the “first-time applicant” declaration, and a threshold dispute over whether the petition is a barred election challenge or a valid Article 32 writ. Two dates matter most: 27 October 2026 (Form 6 legality) and 28 October 2026 (the “first-time applicant” anomaly, ahead of the 30 October Delhi deadline for claims and objections).

1. Case Framework and Procedural Genesis

1.1 The litigants

Petitioners. The petition is reported as Rahul Gandhi v. Election Commission of India, diary no. 64196/2026, filed through Advocate-on-Record S. Prasanna. Reported petitioners include Rahul Gandhi, Mallikarjun Kharge and other INDIA bloc leaders, alongside transparency advocates. The reported reliefs extend beyond Form 6 to a retired-Supreme-Court-judge-led audit committee, record production and interim suspension of SIR.[11][23][49]

Respondents. The ECI, Chief Election Commissioner (CEC) Gyanesh Kumar, and Election Commissioners Sukhbir Singh Sandhu and Vivek Joshi.*

1.2 Filing and procedural defects

The Article 32 petition was filed in October 2026. Reports of registry defects list missing or defective vakalatnamas/authorisation documents, an illegible identity card for Kharge, document/font legibility issues, and a court-fee shortfall of Rs. 220. The registry also reportedly questioned why individual commissioners had been arrayed as respondents. These were procedural defects, not a judicial ruling on the merits.[2][49]

Related report. A Hindu report, supplied among the sources, covers Rahul Gandhi’s plea being not listed for hearing while the defects were yet to be cured.[30]

1.3 Mode of judicial access

After a “March for Democracy” protest in New Delhi on 12 October 2026, opposition leaders sought an informal private audience with Chief Justice Surya Kant to hand over a memorandum.[3][4][5] The Chief Justice declined the requested private audience because related matters were pending, indicating that the petition could be heard with them. This is a procedural explanation, not a rejection on the merits.[50][5][1]

2. Dimension I: Modification of Form 6 versus Portal Addendums

2.1 The petitioners’ submissions

The petitioners challenge a mandatory digital addendum introduced on the ECI’s ECINet portal in July 2026. New applicants, or those updating details, could not submit the statutory Form 6 without completing a genealogical verification section, declaring whether their parents or grandparents were registered in historical SIR rolls.[6] They argue this violates Section 28 of the Representation of the People Act, 1950 (RPA) and the Registration of Electors Rules, 1960. Because Form 6 is a statutory document, they say, making its digital submission conditional on an unlegislated ancestral criterion changes the function of the form itself. That would require formal rule-making by the Law Ministry, Gazette publication and parliamentary review.[6][4]

2.2 The ECI’s counter-arguments

The ECI says the physical, paper-based Form 6 is unaltered in its statutory text, and that the portal questionnaire is an administrative “additional declaration” or data-extension tool for identity verification, not a statutory amendment. It relies on its residual powers under Article 324 and the 27 May 2026 Supreme Court judgment on the Bihar SIR, in which the apex court observed that verification tools confirming voter residency fall within the ECI’s administrative authority to protect roll integrity.[1][8]

2.3 Judicial status

On 12 October 2026, the bench clarified that it had neither approved nor disapproved the changed Form 6 declaration in the Bihar judgment. The remarks arose in the separate Priya Mishra writ petition before CJI Surya Kant and Justices Joymalya Bagchi and V. Mohana. That plea is reported to be heard with similar SIR petitions on 27 October; this should not be described as the INDIA bloc petition’s own listing. The immediate trigger was the ECI’s 26 September press note claiming Supreme Court approval for the Form 6 declaration, a characterisation the Court disputed.[4][6][8][50]

3. Dimension II: Internal ECI Decision-Making

3.1 The petitioners’ submissions

The Indian Express reported that Election Commissioners Sukhbir Singh Sandhu and Vivek Joshi recorded objections at least 14 times over 10 months concerning SIR-related decisions, including changes to Form 6 and data centralisation. The report establishes the existence of reported objections, not by itself a legal breach. Section 18(2) of the Chief Election Commissioner and Other Election Commissioners Act, 2023 says Commission business is to be transacted unanimously as far as possible and, where members differ, by majority. The separate Rakesh Kumar Singh petition alleged unilateral action; the Court sought responses in that matter.[51][52][53]

3.2 The ECI’s counter-arguments

The ECI has described the recorded notes as suggestions or part of deliberation and has said decisions were unanimous. The Indian Express reported that the Commission did not dispute the existence, dates or contents of the 14 notes, while disputing the interpretation placed on them. This is the principal counter-fact and should be read alongside the reported objections.[54][51]

3.3 Historical precedents

  • T.N. Seshan v. Union of India (1995). When the ECI was expanded to three members, the Constitution Bench upheld the multi-member structure and addressed the CEC’s role within it. The phrase “first among equals is often used in commentary, but is not presented here as a direct quotation from the judgment.*
  • Ashok Lavasa dissent (2019). EC Ashok Lavasa recorded disagreement with decisions on Model Code of Conduct complaints during the 2019 general elections. The Commission decided by majority not to include his dissents in its final orders, a point relevant to how internal disagreement is documented.[55]*

4. Dimension III: Mechanics of Deletion

4.1 The petitioners’ submissions

During Delhi’s draft-roll exercise, 47,56,722 electors were deleted from the draft roll under the ASDD categories: absent, shifted, dead and duplicate. Reports also describe about 33 lakh notices issued in Delhi. Nationally, roughly 13.3 crore names were deleted from draft rolls during SIR; draft-roll deletions are not the same as final-roll deletions, because claims, objections and verification can change the final figures.[14][56][57] An existing voter dropped by automated scripts must re-apply on Form 6, which requires a box declaring they are applying “for the first time”. The opposition argues this forces legitimate citizens into a factually incorrect declaration, exposing them to criminal liability for false statements under Section 31 of the RPA, 1950.[12][14][6] A separate transparency concern is whether published lists are machine-readable. The claim should be treated as a reported concern rather than proof of intent; in Bihar, the ECI changed the format after objections about searchable access.[58]

4.2 The ECI’s counter-arguments

The ECI maintains that draft rolls are provisional and designed to be cleaned. Automated scripts identify “logical discrepancies” such as identical identity numbers across booths or anomalous age mappings, and flagging triggers a physical field check by a Booth Level Officer (BLO) rather than immediate, permanent deletion. In states such as Tamil Nadu and Gujarat, many voters flagged by the scripts were restored to final rolls after BLO verification.*

4.3 Judicial status

On 14 October 2026, the bench said it was thinking of passing an order that would take care of the issue and advanced the hearing to 28 October, before the stated 30 October Delhi deadline for claims and objections. The application seeks disclosure of the notice list with reasons and challenges the direction that previously enrolled electors omitted from the draft roll must declare themselves first-time applicants on Form 6. The legal and practical consequences remain for the Court to determine.[13][14][17]

5. Dimension IV: Recent Political, Media and Local Developments

5.1 West Bengal SIR appeals (context)

The facts. The request was to advance the hearing of pending appeals arising from the West Bengal SIR process, not to obtain an interim stay of SIR. The CJI said the matter would be heard on 5 October.[59]

The CJI’s observation. The matter concerned the timing of a hearing on appeals. Avoid reading the listing decision as an interim stay ruling or as a judicial finding about the scale of the administrative burden. The West Bengal appeals hearing is pending for 16 October 2026.[59]

Impact evidence. Press reports put the pending appeals at 18,895, with 90 reportedly decided by the time of the earlier hearing. Those numbers describe a snapshot and should not be treated as the current total without a fresh tribunal list.[59][60]

5.2 Other election-related proceedings (context only)

A separate Supreme Court matter concerning Punjab municipal elections resulted in the setting aside of polls in 50 wards and an order for fresh elections within six weeks. The findings about nomination papers and candidate treatment were attributed to a court-appointed fact-finding commission. The order was dated 28 September 2026 and was reported publicly on 7 October. This case concerns local-body elections, not the nationwide SIR petition.[33][35][71] A separate 2017 ECI exercise invited recognised political parties to demonstrate whether EVMs could be tampered with; contemporary official reporting records that only two parties registered and neither demonstrated tampering within the Commission’s parameters. This historical episode does not resolve the current SIR or Form 6 questions.[40][41]

5.3 The VVPAT judgment and EVM protocols (2024)

Petition. The Association for Democratic Reforms (ADR) sought either 100% manual crossverification of VVPAT slips or a complete return to paper ballots.[42][43]

Verdict (26 April 2024). A bench of Justices Sanjiv Khanna and Dipankar Datta dismissed the plea, holding that suspicions without clear data were unfounded and that counting every slip manually would slow results, cause delay and introduce human error.[44][42]

Protocols in the 26 April 2024 judgment. The Court separately retained the existing random VVPAT-slip count at five polling stations per Assembly constituency/segment and introduced a post-result verification process: candidates placed second or third may seek verification of burnt memory/microcontrollers in up to 5% of EVMs, subject to the stated procedure and cost rules. These are distinct protocols; the 5% memory check is not the existing five-booth random VVPAT count. The claim about a general 2025–26 data-freezing direction is omitted here because the source record supplied does not establish it.[42][61]

6. The Limitation-Period Defence versus the Article 32 Track

A foundational argument in defence of election finality is that no political party filed a formal Election Petition within the statutory timeline after the general elections.[24][25]

6.1 The statutory bar: 45 days

Under Section 81 of the Representation of the People Act, 1951, any challenge to an election’s core validity, counting errors or mass roll manipulation at polling must be brought as an Election Petition.[25] The window is 45 days from the date the returned candidate is elected, and there is no condonation of delay: in Hukumdev Narain Yadav v. Lalit Narain Mishra (1974 AIR 480) the Court held that Sections 4 and 5 of the Limitation Act did not apply to the election petition at issue; Section 10 of the General Clauses Act could nevertheless apply where the final day fell when the court or office was closed. The case should not be paraphrased as saying every form of limitation relief is unavailable in every circumstance.[62][25] I found no reliable reporting establishing that no national or state opposition party filed structural multi-constituency election petitions within the statutory window, so this report does not state that as a documented fact.

6.2 The institutional counter-argument

The ECI/BJP-side argument must be attributed to the specific speaker or filing making it, rather than inferred from the ECI’s general institutional description. The general proposition that completed elections are ordinarily challenged through election petitions under Part VI of the 1951 Act is supported by the statute and case law; the stronger claim that the present petition is politically motivated is an advocacy position, not a judicial finding.[25][63]

6.3 The opposition’s Article 32 justification

To bypass the expired 1951 Act window, the INDIA bloc’s counsel took a different route.[28]

  • Challenging the future, not the past. The petition is not an Election Petition seeking to overturn completed results. It is an Article 32 writ aimed at a new, ongoing administrative event: the nationwide SIR launched in mid-2026.
  • The “Right to Vote” threshold. The petitioners rely on Anoop Baranwal v. Union of India. The majority described voting as a constitutional right but declined to make a final declaration on its precise constitutional status, referring to Kuldip Nayar; avoid saying it definitively recognised voting as a fundamental right. They submit that the mandatory Form 6 ancestral checkboxes and automated deletions are active measures threatening to strip millions of fundamental rights ahead of upcoming state assembly elections.
  • The core distinction. A statutory time bar on past elections, counsel maintain, cannot stop the Supreme Court from reviewing current administrative overreach affecting future democratic cycles.[29][30][31] Related proceedings have been listed for hearing; that listing should not be described as the Court having accepted the petitioners’ legal distinction on the merits.[50]

The 12 October remarks arose in the separate Priya Mishra writ petition, not the INDIA bloc petition.[4][6] On 12 October 2026 the bench said: “Do not put words in our mouth. . . the modified version was not in existence when the court examined the SIR of electoral rolls in Bihar earlier this year.” The petitioners seek scrutiny of the ECI’s decision-making and records; do not state that a record-production order was made unless confirmed by the written order.[8][50]

8. Historical Rulings under Article 324

8.1 Mohinder Singh Gill v. Chief Election Commissioner (1978)

Article 324 is a plenary “reservoir of power” to ensure free and fair elections. The Constitution Bench held that the ECI cannot act against an existing Act of Parliament; where the legislature has laid down election rules, the Commission must work within that framework.[18][16]

8.2 A.C. Jose v. Sivan Pillai (1984)

The Court refined the limits of ECI directives: where Parliament has legislated on a particular aspect of elections, the ECI cannot use Article 324 to bypass, alter or add parallel mandates. Its residual powers apply only to areas left unoccupied by legislation.[18][16]

8.3 Union of India v. Association for Democratic Reforms (2002)

“Conduct of elections” under Article 324 includes the steps needed to maintain the purity of the process. Where legislative rules were silent, the ECI could issue directives, such as requiring candidates’ asset affidavits, provided they directly advanced the goal of a fair election.[18]

8.4 Association for Democratic Reforms v. ECI (27 May 2026)

A bench of Chief Justice Surya Kant and Justice Joymalya Bagchi upheld the ECI’s mandate to conduct the SIR under Article 324 read with Section 21(3) of the RPA, 1950. The ECI has inherent power to cleanse rolls of duplicates, dead citizens and unverified residents, but the Court tied this to safeguards: no name may be permanently deleted without individual notice, a physical hearing and a reasoned order subject to appeal. This ruling is the legal baseline for the current petition.[19][20][7][21][22][23]

9. Recent Operational Policy Adjustments (September–October 2026)

  1. Declaration limited to SIR period (26 September). The Commission decided to limit the declaration to the SIR period; reports said removal became visible by 30 September in states where SIR had concluded. This is a policy adjustment, not a judicial ruling. The press note itself is discussed in contemporaneous reporting; the official direct URL was not independently located.[64][65]
  2. Form 7 limits (order dated 9 October). One elector may submit up to 10 Form 7 applications during claims and objections, and up to five per month during continuous updating. The stated aim is to curb bulk or vexatious applications; the limits do not remove the requirement to follow the prescribed deletion process.[66][67]
  3. BLO home visits. Reporting describes doorstep visits for voters receiving unmapped or logical-discrepancy notices; the evidence located does not support a blanket rule applying specifically to all elderly or remote-area voters.[68]
  4. Delhi verification status. Reports said about 20 lakh of the 33 lakh Delhi notice recipients had been resolved/verified after the Commission’s process changes. “Resolved” is used rather than “reinstated”: verification does not necessarily mean a voter was restored to the roll. A civil-society investigation has questioned the adequacy and transparency of the process.[69][70]

11. Notes on Sources and Discrepancies

  • Court fee and defects. The better-matched reports state a Rs. 220 shortfall. The older Rs. 200 figure in one URL slug is not repeated as an alternative. Reported defects included authorisation/vakalatnama issues, Kharge’s ID, document legibility/fonts and a registry query about arraying individual commissioners.
  • Distinct proceedings. The INDIA bloc petition, the Priya Mishra Form 6 writ, the Rakesh Kumar Singh petition alleging unilateral decision-making, and the Delhi application about first-time declarations are related in subject matter but are not interchangeable. Hearing dates and observations are attributed to the proceeding in which they arose.
  • Draft versus final rolls. The Delhi 47,56,722 figure concerns draft-roll deletions under ASDD categories. National figures around 13.3 crore also concern draft-stage changes; neither figure alone establishes the final number of eligible voters excluded after claims, objections and verification.
  • VVPAT. The 26 April 2024 judgment addresses both the existing random VVPAT count at five polling stations and a separate post-result memory/microcontroller verification procedure covering up to 5% of EVMs. No general 2025–26 data-freezing claim is retained without a specific order.
  • Residual sourcing gaps. The exact text of the 26 September ECI press note, the written 9 October Form 7 order, and the 28 September Punjab order should be preferred over news summaries where available. The ECI press note is discussed by a secondary source here; the direct official note URL was not verified in this pass. The petition diary number and counsel details are reported, not independently verified against a publicly accessible docket. The report does not claim that the Court has accepted either side’s merits arguments.
